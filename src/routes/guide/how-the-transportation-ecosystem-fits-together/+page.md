_Which rules your city must follow, and which it can choose to ignore_

In 2010, a group of Los Angeles residents with mobility disabilities sued their city over its broken sidewalks, missing curb ramps, and blocked crosswalks.<Citation index={1} /> They didn't cite a design guide. They cited federal disability law.<Citation index={1} />

In 2016, a federal court approved the settlement: Los Angeles would spend about $1.37 billion over 30 years making its sidewalks and crosswalks accessible.<Citation index={1} /> The money pays for curb ramps, repairs to tree-root damage and uneven pavement, and clearing obstructions, with a share set aside for residents' own requests.<Citation index={2} />

A design guide can recommend a wider, smoother sidewalk. Only a law can make a city build one. This page is about telling the two apart.

## Three kinds of documents

Every document that shapes your street is one of three things:

<div class="cw-tiles">

- **Rules that bind.** Laws and regulations. If your city is subject to them, following them isn't optional.
- **Guidance professionals follow by choice.** Manuals and design guides written by engineers and planners. Cities often adopt them, but nothing forces them to.
- **Arguments advocates make.** Movements and nonprofits building public pressure for a different kind of street. Their power shows up in what voters demand and what gets funded.

</div>

The name on the cover won't tell you which is which. AASHTO's Green Book calls itself *A Policy on Geometric Design*, yet federal regulation makes it the required standard for National Highway System projects.<Citation index={3} /> NACTO's *Urban Street Design Guide* comes from an association of cities and transit agencies, and FHWA treats it as one of many sources to inform design, not a requirement.<Citation index={4} /><Citation index={5} />

## Why you should care

<div class="cw-tiles">

- **When the city says no.** "The manual won't allow it" is sometimes true. Sometimes the line being cited is only a recommendation, which an engineer may set aside when judgment or study supports it.<Citation index={6} />
- **At a public meeting.** Citing a binding rule asks the city to comply. Citing guidance asks it to choose. Both can work, but they are different requests.
- **When you read a pattern.** Every reference in the Commonway System carries a tier chip: Legal / regulatory, Evidence-based, or Precedent-based. This page explains the difference those chips mark.
- **When a name gets dropped.** MUTCD, PROWAG, NACTO, AASHTO. After this page, you'll know which ones carry weight where you live.

</div>

## How guidance becomes law

<div class="cw-options">

**Adoption turns a guide into a rule.** AASHTO is an association of state transportation departments, not a federal agency.<Citation index={7} /> Its Green Book becomes binding because federal regulation incorporates it by reference for National Highway System projects. Off that system, federal-aid projects follow state laws and standards instead.<Citation index={3} />

**Some guidelines wait years for adoption.** The U.S. Access Board issued its Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines (PROWAG), covering sidewalks, crosswalks, curb ramps, and pedestrian signals, as a final rule in August 2023.<Citation index={8} /> The General Services Administration adopted them in July 2024. The Department of Transportation adopted them for transit stops, effective January 17, 2025.<Citation index={9} /> The Department of Justice, which would make them enforceable for city and state sidewalks under the ADA, had not adopted them as of February 2026.<Citation index={10} />

**Even binding rules have soft parts.** The Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) is the national standard for signs, signals, and pavement markings. States adopt it as is, add a supplement, or write their own manual in substantial conformance, and must adopt federal changes within two years.<Citation index={11} /> Inside the manual, only Standard statements, written with *shall*, are mandatory. Guidance statements, written with *should*, allow deviations when engineering judgment or study supports them. Option statements, written with *may*, carry no requirement at all.<Citation index={6} />

**Advocacy can become policy.** Complete Streets is an approach championed by an advocacy coalition: plan, design, and build streets so everyone who uses them can do so safely.<Citation index={12} /> By the end of 2020, almost 1,600 communities had adopted Complete Streets policies.<Citation index={13} />

</div>

:::note
**Quick test:** When someone cites a document, ask two questions. Has a government adopted it where you live? And does the line they're citing say *shall*, *should*, or *may*?<Citation index={6} /><Citation index={11} />
:::

## Fair questions

<div class="cw-options">

**If NACTO isn't law, why would my city follow it?** Because FHWA backs it. In 2013, FHWA expressed support for a flexible approach to bicycle and pedestrian facility design and noted that most treatments in NACTO's bikeway guide are allowed or not precluded by the MUTCD.<Citation index={5} /> In 2014, FHWA extended that support to NACTO's *Urban Street Design Guide* as one of many sources for planning and design.<Citation index={5} />

**Isn't the Green Book the law?** On National Highway System projects, effectively yes, though federal rules still allow documented design exceptions where conditions warrant. Elsewhere, it binds only where a state's own standards adopt it.<Citation index={3} />

**Aren't advocacy groups biased?** They argue for an outcome; that's their job. The Commonway System cites advocates for what they argue, and looks to regulation and research for proof. The tier chip on each reference shows which is which.

**Why use Dutch sources for American streets?** Because the Dutch have a deep bench of both research and practice. SWOV has researched road safety since 1962, and CROW publishes the practical guidelines Dutch designers use.<Citation index={14} /><Citation index={15} /> The Commonway System adapts their guidance to U.S. law rather than copying it.

</div>

## Who's who

Grouped by how much authority each one carries.

### Binding regulation

| Organization | What it is and publishes | How the Commonway System uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) | Federal agency. Its MUTCD is the national standard for traffic control devices.<Citation index={11} /> | Patterns stay consistent with the MUTCD. Confirm your state's version. |
| U.S. Access Board | Federal agency. PROWAG sets accessibility guidelines for sidewalks, crosswalks, curb ramps, and pedestrian signals, enforceable where adopted.<Citation index={8} /><Citation index={9} /> | Accessibility details in patterns. |
| U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) | Federal agency. Green infrastructure, such as permeable pavement and plant or soil systems, can be used to meet Clean Water Act requirements.<Citation index={16} /> | The regulatory backdrop for stormwater retrofits. |

### Professional guidance

| Organization | What it is and publishes | How the Commonway System uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NACTO | Association of more than 100 North American cities and transit agencies. Publishes the *Urban Street Design Guide*, *Urban Bikeway Design Guide*, and *City Limits*.<Citation index={4} /> | A core source for urban streets. |
| AASHTO | Association of the transportation departments of all 50 states, DC, and Puerto Rico. Sets technical standards for highway design, including the Green Book.<Citation index={3} /><Citation index={7} /> | Geometry and safety guidance for vehicle-facing design. |
| ITE | Volunteer association of more than 18,000 engineers, planners, and consultants in more than 78 countries. Publishes standards and recommended practices.<Citation index={17} /> | Professional consensus, not regulation. |

### Advocacy

| Organization | What it is and argues | How the Commonway System uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong Towns | Member-powered movement arguing that America's postwar "Suburban Experiment" should give way to more financially durable places, built through small, local steps.<Citation index={18} /> | Street decisions as financial decisions. |
| Congress for the New Urbanism (CNU) | Nonprofit with about 2,600 members championing walkable urbanism over sprawl, guided by its Charter of the New Urbanism.<Citation index={19} /> | Urban form and walkable design. |
| Vision Zero Network | Nonprofit campaign helping communities eliminate traffic deaths and severe injuries, using the Safe System approach.<Citation index={20} /> That approach starts from the premise that people make mistakes, and that those mistakes should never lead to death.<Citation index={21} /> | Forgiving design over relying on perfect drivers. |
| Project for Public Spaces (PPS) | Since 1975, has practiced placemaking: designing parks, plazas, markets, and streets with the people who use them, in more than 3,500 communities.<Citation index={22} /> | The street as public space, not just a traffic problem. |
| National Complete Streets Coalition (Smart Growth America) | Nonprofit, nonpartisan alliance working to make streets safe for all users the default.<Citation index={12} /> | The multimodal default CS builds on. |
| Create Streets | UK social enterprise, design practice, and think tank started by Nicholas Boys Smith in 2012. Promotes "gentle density" and helped shape national policy through the Building Better, Building Beautiful Commission, which Boys Smith co-chaired.<Citation index={23} /> | An international echo. Its focus is building form, distinct from CS density tiers. |

## Where the Commonway System fits

The Commonway System doesn't outrank any of these organizations. FHWA still sets the rules it sets. The Green Book is still the geometric baseline most engineers learned. Strong Towns still makes the case that street decisions are financial decisions.

What the Commonway System does is narrower. It takes guidance these organizations publish separately, in their own formats and for their own audiences, and reconciles it into one set of patterns. You don't need to know that curb ramp rules come from PROWAG, that bikeway geometry draws on NACTO and CROW, and that the safety philosophy traces to Vision Zero. The pattern has already done that work.

For a newcomer, that means starting with a pattern instead of a research project. For a professional, it means the cross-referencing is already done.

## What you can do

When someone cites a document to say no, or to say yes:

- Ask who adopted it where you live, and when.
- Ask for the exact line, and whether it says *shall*, *should*, or *may*.
- If it's guidance, ask why the city chose that guidance over other guidance FHWA also supports.

Knowing what a good fix looks like is half the job. Getting it approved is the other half. [How Road Changes Actually Happen](/guide/make-it-happen/) covers who to ask and how.
