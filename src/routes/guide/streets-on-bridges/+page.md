_Why a street's sidewalks and bike lanes should cross the bridge with it_

Portland's Hawthorne Bridge opened in 1910 and is the oldest working vertical lift bridge in the United States. In 1999, Multnomah County replaced its steel deck. While the deck was out, the county widened the bridge's path from 6 to 10 ft and added sidewalks and ramps at the west end.<Citation index={1} />

That year, about 3,150 people biked across the bridge each day. By 2016, about 8,000 did, alongside 30,000 cars and trucks and 800 buses. Of all the work done on the bridge over the years, the biggest rise in walking and biking followed that one.<Citation index={1} />

The wider path wasn't a separate project. The county had started planning the rehabilitation with 14 agencies in 1996, and the path went into the deck work it was doing anyway.<Citation index={1} /> A bridge gets that kind of chance only once in decades.

## Why you should care

<div class="cw-tiles">

- **There's no way around.** A bridge is often the only crossing of a river, rail line or freeway for a long way. Without room on it, a walking or biking route ends or detours.<Citation index={1} />
- **The next chance is decades away.** Major bridge work comes years or decades apart.<Citation index={1} /> Minneapolis expects its Franklin Avenue Bridge rehabilitation to add 50 to 75 years of life.<Citation index={1} />
- **It costs less during bridge work.** Building walking and biking space while the bridge is being worked on almost always costs less than adding it later or building a separate bridge.<Citation index={1} />
- **Many bridges are coming due.** In 2025, 41,685 of the nation's 624,193 bridges were in poor condition.<Citation index={2} />

</div>

## How it works

A bridge is a piece of the street, not a different kind of road. For urban collectors and arterials, AASHTO recommends a new bridge at least as wide, curb to curb, as the street leading to it. FHWA adds that where the approach has sidewalks, carrying them across the bridge is important.<Citation index={3} /> Wisconsin DOT asks for bike lanes or shoulders on a bridge's approaches too, ideally for 1,000 ft on either side of a major bridge.<Citation index={4} />

Federal law backs this up. When federal money helps replace or rehabilitate a bridge deck, and people may walk or bike at each end, the bridge must be rebuilt to accommodate them safely if that can be done at reasonable cost.<Citation index={5} /> Wisconsin DOT reports that FHWA set reasonable cost at no more than 20 percent of the larger project.<Citation index={4} /><Citation unsourced />

The same holds where a street passes under a rail line. Wisconsin DOT counts these crossings with the rest: accommodation matters whether the road goes over the tracks or under them.<Citation index={4} /> Under a rail viaduct, though, the structure is usually the hard part to change. In Bend, Oregon, four-lane Greenwood Avenue, carrying about 41,000 vehicles a day, passes under two railroad bridges on sidewalks about 4 ft wide, with no bike lanes. Replacing the railroad bridges was judged likely too costly; the temporary track alone could exceed $1 million. The engineers instead recommended taking the street to two lanes, which made room for 8 ft sidewalks and 6 ft buffered bike lanes for about $829,000.<Citation index={6} /> Under a viaduct, the street usually changes because the structure can't.

That change hasn't happened yet. Bend still lists the 2016 study as needing further study.<Citation index={8} /> In 2024 the city did take Greenwood to two lanes with bike lanes, but only west of Second Street, short of the underpass. East of Third Street, Greenwood becomes a state highway, so the Oregon DOT has to be part of any change.<Citation index={9} /> A bridge or underpass often belongs to someone other than the city, which is one reason these fixes take years.

## What the Commonway System does

A bridge, or a rail viaduct a street passes under, carries the same pattern as the street on either side. Its sidewalks, bike lanes, separation and speed continue across the structure unchanged. Where a bridge has to be narrower than its approaches, the width comes out of travel lanes and shoulders, not out of the walking and biking space. Every Local, Collector and Arterial pattern says so.

<div class="cw-fields">

**On an existing bridge.** [Bridge deck reallocation (RFT-CDR-12)](/retrofits/corridor/bridge-deck-reallocation/) takes width from narrower lanes, a median, a shoulder or a travel lane, without rebuilding the bridge.

**When a bridge is rebuilt.** [Bridge replacement cross-section (RFT-CDR-13)](/retrofits/corridor/bridge-replacement-cross-section/) builds the approach streets' pattern into the new deck.

**Under a rail viaduct.** A [Four-to-three lane road diet (RFT-CDR-01)](/retrofits/corridor/four-to-three-lane-road-diet/) or [Lane diet (RFT-CDR-03)](/retrofits/corridor/lane-diet/) finds the room, as in Bend.

**Where no crossing exists.** The [Barrier-crossing / network reconnection retrofit (RFT-NET-03)](/retrofits/network/barrier-crossing-network-reconnection-retrofit/) adds one, as a [Grade-Separated Walk and Bike Crossing (INT-GSW-16)](/patterns/intersections/grade-separated-walk-bike-crossing/). A [Walk and Bike Overpass (INT-OVP-17)](/patterns/intersections/walk-bike-overpass/) is Situational / Discouraged on bike routes.

**Over a sunken freeway.** A [Freeway cap (RFT-NET-04)](/retrofits/network/freeway-cap/) lets people cross at ground level.

</div>

## Fair questions

**Isn't a wider bridge much more expensive?**
Width costs more on a bridge than on a street, and AASHTO accepts narrower widths on long, costly spans. It also says economy alone shouldn't decide a bridge's width.<Citation index={7} /> The Commonway System takes any savings from travel lanes and shoulders first.

**What if the street has no sidewalk or bike lane yet?**
Build for the network that's planned. A bridge can be upgraded before the sidewalks or greenways around it exist, on the understanding that the connections will follow.<Citation index={1} />

**Wouldn't a separate walk and bike bridge be better?**
Sometimes, where none exists. But adding the space during bridge work almost always costs less than a standalone bridge, and it keeps the route where the street already goes.<Citation index={1} />

**Does this apply to freeways?**
Not in detail: freeways sit outside the guidebook's design scope. A large highway bridge can still carry a walking and biking path, as the Richmond-San Rafael Bridge in California was set to do.<Citation index={1} />

## What you can do

Bridge projects are planned years ahead, and the walking and biking space is easiest to win early. Left until final design or construction, the options narrow.<Citation index={1} />

- Find out who owns the bridge. It is often a state DOT, a county or an authority rather than the city. See [When Jurisdiction Doesn't Follow the Pattern](/guide/make-it-happen/jurisdiction-variants/).
- Ask when the deck is due for work, and comment during the alternatives analysis and public meetings, when the cross-section is still open.<Citation index={1} />
- If federal money is involved, cite 23 U.S.C. 217(e).<Citation index={5} />
- Ask for the pattern of the street on either side, not the minimum.

See [How Road Changes Actually Happen](/guide/make-it-happen/) for how to raise this at a public meeting.
