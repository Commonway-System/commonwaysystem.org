_Why the sign doesn't set the speed_

In 2009, Los Angeles moved to raise the speed limit on Zelzah Avenue between Chatsworth and Nordhoff streets from 35 to 40 mph. A city survey had found most drivers already going faster. California tied radar enforcement to that kind of survey, so the transportation department said the limit had to rise.<Citation index={1} /><Citation index={2} />

A Cal State Northridge student told the campus paper that raising the limit would only encourage more speeding, and that the next survey would push it up again.<Citation index={1} />

In 2018, after another survey, the city raised the same stretch to 45.<Citation index={2} /><Citation index={3} /> NACTO notes no design or land-use changes in between. Other factors may have played a part, but the case suggests drivers' speeds drift up to follow the posted limit.<Citation index={2} /> In 2022, after California gave cities more room, Los Angeles brought it back down to 40.<Citation index={3} /><Citation index={4} />

The sign kept chasing the drivers. The drivers were following the street. This page is about that gap.

## Three speeds, three deciders

Every street has three speeds, and different people set each one.<Citation index={5} />

<GuideDiagram priority src="/diagrams/design-speed-diagram.png" alt="Commonway System design speed diagram showing the relationship between design speed, operating speed, and posted speed limit." width={1280} height={720} />

<div class="cw-tiles">

- **Design speed** is set by engineers. It is a chosen number used to size the road's curves, sight lines, lane widths, and other geometry.<Citation index={6} /><Citation index={7} /> It appears on the plans, not on a sign.
- **Operating speed** is set by drivers. Engineers usually measure it as the 85th-percentile speed: the speed that 85 percent of free-flowing drivers go at or below.<Citation index={8} />
- **The posted speed limit** is set by a state or local agency. Federal guidance says it should fall within 5 mph of the 85th-percentile speed, and every state agency in one FHWA-cited survey used that measure.<Citation index={9} />

</div>

These three numbers often disagree.<Citation index={5} /> The strongest link among them runs between how fast people drive and the posted limit. Neither relates to design speed with anywhere near that confidence.<Citation index={10} />

## Why you should care

<div class="cw-tiles">

- **Your neighbors' lives.** A person on foot hit at 23 mph has about a 1 in 10 chance of dying. At 32 mph it is 1 in 4; at 42 mph, 1 in 2.<Citation index={11} />
- **Your parents.** A 70-year-old hit at 25 mph faces about the same risk of severe injury or death as a 30-year-old hit at 35.<Citation index={11} />
- **Your street.** A speed survey can push a street's limit up even when nothing about the street has changed.<Citation index={2} /><Citation index={9} />
- **Your sidewalks and corners.** Higher design speeds bring wider lanes, wider turning curves, guardrails, broad clear zones, and lost parking, all of which make a street harder to walk.<Citation index={12} />

</div>

## How it happens

<div class="cw-options">

**Roads are built for more speed than their design speed.** Geometry standards assume near-worst-case conditions, such as slow reaction times, so a road built to them carries more speed in good conditions.<Citation index={8} /><Citation index={9} /> Designers are also told to exceed the minimums where they can. The result is an *inferred design speed*, the fastest speed the finished geometry supports, often well above the number on the plans.<Citation index={7} /><Citation index={9} />

**Drivers follow the road, not the plans.** People pick a speed from what they see: lane width, curves, sight lines, driveways, parked cars, people walking.<Citation index={9} /><Citation index={10} /><Citation index={13} />

**Nothing ties the numbers together.** A 1985 FHWA memo said design speed should equal or exceed the posted limit. Later FHWA memos softened that, calling the posted limit an operating decision for the road's owner. In 2001, AASHTO's Green Book redefined design speed as simply a number chosen to size the road's geometry, and its guidance on choosing one says nothing about speed limits. In 2015, FHWA stated that no regulation establishes a direct link between them.<Citation index={7} />

**The limit ratchets up.** Posted limits are set near the 85th-percentile speed, so when a street invites faster driving, the next survey raises the limit.<Citation index={9} /> FHWA itself warns that raising limits to match speeds may push speeds higher still, in a cycle of escalation.<Citation index={9} /> In one FHWA example, a Pennsylvania highway opened at 40 mph and was raised to 50 after a review of actual speeds; both exceeded its design speed.<Citation index={9} /> NACTO sums up the pattern as higher speeds and higher limits over time.<Citation index={2} />

</div>

## How strong is the evidence

:::note
The faster a car is going when it hits someone on foot, the likelier that person dies: about 10 percent at 23 mph, 25 percent at 32 mph, and 50 percent at 42 mph.<Citation index={11} /> A street's design, more than its sign, sets how fast people drive.<Citation index={9} /><Citation index={10} />
:::

These figures come from a 2011 AAA Foundation study of U.S. crashes, weighted to correct older studies' habit of oversampling the worst crashes, so they run lower than some widely quoted older estimates. Even so, risk climbs steeply across the speeds most city streets are posted at.<Citation index={11} />

The design side is less tidy. A federal research project found no reliable link between design speed and operating speed, but found that driveway density, medians, parking, and pedestrian activity did affect how fast people drove.<Citation index={10} /> FHWA's own guide notes that the design process has no systematic consistency check and is suspected of encouraging speeds above the design speed.<Citation index={9} />

## Fair questions

<div class="cw-options">

**Won't drivers just ignore a lower sign?** Often, yes. FHWA reports that research has repeatedly found changing the posted limit alone does little to actual speeds.<Citation index={9} /> Newer studies are more hopeful: after Toronto cut local-street limits from 40 to 30 km/h, pedestrian crashes fell 28 percent and serious and fatal injuries 67 percent, though the drop was not statistically larger than on comparison streets.<Citation index={14} /> A sign works best when the street agrees with it.

**Why not just enforce the limit?** Enforcement works near the officer or camera, and the effect fades with distance and time.<Citation index={9} /> Street design keeps working when no one is watching.<Citation index={2} />

**Isn't the 85th percentile a fair way to set limits?** It assumes most drivers choose a reasonable speed, and engineers have relied on it since the 1930s.<Citation index={8} /> NACTO notes that the research behind it came from rural two-lane highways, not streets where people walk and cross.<Citation index={2} /> On streets that invite speeding, it feeds the ratchet above.<Citation index={9} />

**Isn't it safe to drive faster than a road's design speed?** On many rural highways, often yes. Design criteria include a safety margin, and FHWA says a posted limit above the inferred design speed is not necessarily unsafe.<Citation index={7} /><Citation index={8} /> On streets, the risk falls mostly on people outside the car, and their odds worsen fast as speed rises.<Citation index={11} />

**Won't slower streets make my trip longer?** Usually by little. When Seattle took a mile of Rainier Avenue South from four lanes to three and cut its limit to 25 mph, evening car trips along the corridor took 54 seconds to 1 minute 21 seconds longer, and northbound evening buses got 3 minutes faster.<Citation index={15} />

</div>

## What works

<div class="cw-options">

**Build the speed into the street.** FHWA calls this a self-enforcing road: one designed so drivers choose the intended speed on their own. In that approach, the target speed sets the design speed and becomes the posted limit.<Citation index={13} /> NACTO's urban street guidance says the same: target speed, design speed, and posted speed should match.<Citation index={12} />

**Example: Rainier Avenue South, Seattle.** In 2015, Seattle converted a mile of this four-lane arterial to one lane each way plus a center turn lane, and lowered the limit from 30 to 25 mph to match the project's 25 mph design speed.<Citation index={15} /> At one count point, the share of northbound drivers speeding fell from 84 to 40 percent, and northbound drivers going over 40 mph fell 81 percent. In the first year, crashes fell 15 percent, injury crashes 30 percent, and crashes involving people walking or biking 40 percent, with no serious-injury crashes against a prior average of 9 a year.<Citation index={15} />

**Let cities set limits for safety.** California's AB 43, in 2021, let cities cut an extra 5 mph on their most dangerous streets and where many people walk and bike, and hold a limit steady when a new survey would have raised it.<Citation index={4} /> Los Angeles then approved 5 mph cuts on about 177 miles of streets; before the law, it had been required to raise limits on 200 miles.<Citation index={3} /> NACTO's *City Limits* gives cities a method that sets limits from conflict and activity levels rather than from current driving speeds.<Citation index={2} />

</div>

## What the Commonway System does

**Design speed always equals the posted speed limit.**

On every Local, Collector, and Arterial pattern in this guidebook, the speed on the sign is the speed the street is built for and the speed people are meant to drive, all at once, by design. It is not a target to work toward. The street's lanes, curves, and sight lines make any faster speed feel wrong to drive.<Citation index={12} /><Citation index={13} />

| Classification | Speed rule |
| --- | --- |
| Local | 20 mph maximum, no exceptions |
| Collector | 30 mph; 35 mph only with rigid, crash-protective bike separation |
| Arterial | 30 to 55 mph by density tier, minus 5 mph for noise or frequent access (see below) |
| Freeway | Left to existing federal and state systems |

### Local: 20 mph, no exceptions

The Stockholm Declaration, agreed at the 2020 global ministerial conference on road safety, calls for a 30 km/h (about 20 mph) maximum where people walking or biking mix often with traffic, except where strong evidence shows a higher speed is safe.<Citation index={16} /> CS holds Local streets to 20 mph with no exception clause, a stricter rule. A struck pedestrian's risk of severe injury reaches 10 percent at 16 mph, and risk of death reaches 10 percent at 23 mph.<Citation index={11} /> Local geometry must make 20 mph the comfortable speed, and no Local pattern may be designed to a higher inferred speed. See [Default Local Street (LOC-DEF-01)](/patterns/local-streets/default/).

### Collector: 30 mph, or 35 with rigid separation

CS holds Collectors to 30 mph. It allows 35 mph only where bike facilities have rigid, crash-protective separation, such as a concrete barrier or permanent curb, not flexible posts.

- Seattle's street design manual prefers curb medians where speeds are expected to exceed 30 mph, treats flexible delineators as temporary, and calls for permanent separation where volumes and speeds are higher.<Citation index={17} />
- FHWA's separated bike lane guide treats delineator posts and rigid barriers as different options, chosen partly by traffic speed, and notes the posts' durability problems.<Citation index={18} />
- Dutch practice draws the line at the same speed: distributor roads at this speed class call for physical separation between cars and bikes.<Citation index={19} />
- FHWA's bikeway guide notes that crash and death risk for people walking and biking rise sharply once vehicle speeds pass 25 mph.<Citation index={20} />

See [Default Collector (COL-DEF-01)](/patterns/collectors/default/).

### Arterial and Freeway

Arterial speeds start from a density-tier baseline with two possible adjustments, detailed in [How Arterial speeds are set](#how-arterial-speeds-are-set) below. Freeways ([Freeway Default (FRE-DEF-01)](/patterns/freeways/default/), [Expressway (FRE-EXP-02)](/patterns/freeways/expressway/), [Beltway or Ring Road (FRE-BLT-03)](/patterns/freeways/beltway-ring-road/)) sit outside CS's detailed design scope. AASHTO's Interstate standards and each state DOT's speed-setting authority already govern them, and CS respects that system rather than duplicating it.

## What you can do

When a speed survey proposes raising a limit near you, ask:

- What is this street's design speed, and what speed does its geometry actually support?
- If drivers are going too fast, why change the sign instead of the street?
- Does our state let the city set limits for safety rather than from current speeds?

When a street is repaved or rebuilt, ask whether the new lanes, curves, and crossings will match the posted limit. See [How Road Changes Actually Happen](/guide/make-it-happen/) for how to raise these at a public meeting.

## How Arterial speeds are set

No existing framework sets Arterial design speed the way CS needs, so this is original CS synthesis, built from FHWA's density principle, NACTO's speed guidance, Dutch noise policy, and Virginia's access-spacing logic. Every piece is cited.

**Baseline by density tier.** FHWA guidance holds that a rural highway warrants a higher design speed than an urban one, and that for roads other than freeways and major arterials, design speed should typically fall as land use density rises.<Citation index={21} /> NACTO goes further for urban streets, arterials included: design at or below the target speed, and keep higher speeds for limited-access freeways and highways.<Citation index={12} /> Its maximum target speed for urban arterials is 35 mph.<Citation index={12} /> NACTO's guide is written for city streets, so CS applies that cap to its Urban and Core tiers and sets higher baselines for the lower-density Suburban and Compact tiers. CS's six [Density Tiers](/guide/density-tiers/) give the principle a structured baseline:

| Density Tier | Baseline Arterial Speed |
| --- | --- |
| Undeveloped / Rural | 55 mph |
| Suburban | 45 mph |
| Compact | 40 mph |
| Urban | 35 mph |
| Core | 30 mph |

Core's baseline meets Collector's ceiling by design. In CS's densest tier, an Arterial's surroundings are closer to a Collector's than to a rural Arterial's, even though it still carries through traffic.

<div class="cw-options cw-options--pair">

**Noise modifier: minus 5 mph.** Where an Arterial segment runs within the noise zone (defined below) of homes, schools, or healthcare, subtract 5 mph from the baseline. Both sides of the Atlantic treat lower speed as a noise tool. Until 2024, the Dutch Noise Abatement Act (Wet geluidhinder) set road-noise limits and required abatement where they were exceeded.<Citation index={22} /> The N325 Pleyroute in Arnhem is reported to carry its 80 km/h limit for environmental and noise reasons.<Citation index={23} /> In the U.S., 23 CFR 772, the federal noise rule behind every state DOT's noise policy, lists modified speed limits among the traffic management measures eligible for noise abatement.<Citation index={24} /> AASHTO's environmental guidance adds that a speed cut of about 20 mph is needed for a readily noticeable drop in noise.<Citation index={25} /> CS's 5 mph step is smaller because it is one of two contributing modifiers, not a standalone noise fix.

**Noise-sensitive adjacency is a distance, set the Dutch way.** Until 2024, Article 74 of the Dutch act gave every road a noise zone on each side, measured from the road's centerline and sized by lane count and by urban or rural setting:<Citation index={22} />

</div>

| Lanes | Urban zone | Rural zone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1-2 | 660 ft (200 m) | 820 ft (250 m) |
| 3-4 | 1,150 ft (350 m) | 1,310 ft (400 m) |
| 5+ | 1,150 ft (350 m) | 1,970 ft (600 m) |

A noise-sensitive use inside the zone qualifies for the modifier; one outside it does not. Roads posted at 30 km/h and woonerven had no zone at all.<Citation index={22} /> CS follows the same logic in limiting this modifier to Arterials. CS adopts the table directly, converted to feet, measured from the Arterial's centerline, and keyed to the segment's own lane count.

FHWA's approach is not a distance rule. Under 23 CFR 772, agencies model predicted noise at specific receptor points with FHWA's Traffic Noise Model, and abatement is considered only when a receptor's predicted level approaches or exceeds the federal Noise Abatement Criteria.<Citation index={24} /> That is a case-by-case standard that needs an acoustic model for each project, not a rule someone can check with a tape measure.

CS takes the Dutch rule over the federal standard on purpose. The law-and-economics literature frames the tradeoff this way: rules cost more to write, but standards cost more for people to interpret before acting and for officials to apply afterward, so rules win when the same question comes up often.<Citation index={26} /> Guidance on drafting local noise ordinances reaches a similar view: numeric ordinances give objective, predictable standards, while vague ones invite uneven enforcement.<Citation index={27} /> Rules are also cheaper to enforce, because applying them doesn't require case-specific facts, though the same study argues that where enforcement is thin, a standard's vagueness can sometimes raise compliance.<Citation index={28} /> A pattern book meant to be used without an acoustic study for every Arterial segment fits the case for a rule: it gives the same answer every time.

**Access modifier: minus 5 mph.** Where an Arterial segment has three or more crossing points per quarter mile (intersecting streets, driveways, or curb cuts; on average, one every 440 feet or closer), subtract 5 mph from the baseline. That is NACTO's line for a high density of crossing points, one of the inputs to its speed-limit matrix.<Citation index={2} /> Crash risk rises with access density: research summarized for the National Cooperative Highway Research Program found each added access point per mile raised a road's crash rate about 4 percent.<Citation index={29} /> The modifier also inverts the Virginia Department of Transportation's logic, which requires wider spacing between intersections and entrances at higher speed limits.<Citation index={30} /> CS reads the relationship in reverse: tight spacing is itself evidence the speed should come down.

**The two modifiers do not stack.** If a segment qualifies for both, only the larger reduction applies. This follows how three fields handle overlapping risk factors:

- NACTO's *City Limits* sets urban speeds from a matrix of conflict density and activity level, not by adding scores together.<Citation index={2} />
- FHWA treats design speed as one of a small set of controlling design criteria, with each departure justified through its own documented design exception.<Citation index={31} />
- FHWA's crash modification guidance warns that unless countermeasures act fully independently, multiplying their effects likely overstates the combined benefit.<Citation index={32} /> Texas DOT's safety manual caps combinations at three for that reason, and names the fix for overlapping factors: the dominant effect method, which applies only the single most effective factor and is the simplest, most conservative approach.<Citation index={33} />

CS's noise and access modifiers are not independent. Both stand in for the same thing: how much a road's surroundings, not its through-traffic job, should pull its speed down. Applying only the larger follows the same conservative logic safety practice already uses.

**Floor: 30 mph.** The result never drops below 30 mph except through a documented exception, the same default-plus-documented-exception structure CS uses for roundabout-first intersection control and its crash-investigation process. Because Core's baseline is already 30 mph, a Core Arterial that qualifies for either modifier needs a documented exception to go lower.
