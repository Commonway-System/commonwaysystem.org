Uses a bridge's deck replacement, rehabilitation or full replacement to build the walking and biking space the street on either side has or plans, since the chance comes only once in decades.

## Overview

Major work on a bridge comes years or decades apart, and a bridge outlasts the typical stretch of road.<Citation index={1} /> Whatever cross-section goes back on the deck is the one the crossing keeps for a generation. Of the 624,193 bridges in the National Bridge Inventory in 2025, 41,685 (6.7 percent) were rated in poor condition.<Citation index={2} /> Each of them is a coming chance to get the cross-section right.

## Applicability and thresholds

Any bridge due for a new deck, rehabilitation or replacement where people may walk or bike at either end. Federal law sets the floor: when a federally funded project replaces or rehabilitates a bridge deck on a highway where people may walk or bike at each end, the bridge must be rebuilt to accommodate them safely if that can be done at reasonable cost.<Citation index={3} /> On width, AASHTO recommends that a new bridge on an urban collector or arterial be at least as wide, curb to curb, as the approach street, and FHWA notes that sidewalk continuity over the bridge is important.<Citation index={4} /> Plan for the network the approaches will have, not only the one they have today.<Citation index={1} />

## Evidence and effectiveness

When Portland rehabilitated the Hawthorne Bridge in 1999, it widened the multi-use path from 6 to 10 ft. About 3,150 people biked across each day in 1999; by 2016 it was about 8,000, and the biggest rise in walking and biking on the bridge followed that project. In Ithaca, the Thurston Avenue Bridge was widened by 12 ft to carry 10 ft sidewalks and 5 ft bike lanes. In Minneapolis, the Franklin Avenue Bridge rehabilitation adds a 14 ft separated multi-use path and extends the bridge's life by 50 to 75 years.<Citation index={1} /> Building the facility during bridge work almost always costs less than adding it later or building a separate walk and bike bridge.<Citation index={1} />

## Getting it built

Permanent by nature: the cross-section is set in the structural design. The walking and biking space has to be in the project from the start; left until final design or construction, the options narrow.<Citation index={1} /> Where the deck is wider than today's traffic needs, the extra width can open as paint and posts and be made permanent later, as with [Bridge deck reallocation](/retrofits/corridor/bridge-deck-reallocation/) <IdPill id="RFT-CDR-12" />. See [Construction Methodology & The Build Spectrum](/guide/construction-methodology-spectrum/).

</div>
<div class="cw-fact">

## Who can move this

The bridge owner, often a state DOT, county or authority rather than the city, and the federal funding behind the project, which brings the 217(e) requirement with it.<Citation index={3} /> The project's alternatives analysis and public meetings are where walking and biking space gets added or lost.<Citation index={1} /> See [Jurisdiction Variants](/guide/make-it-happen/jurisdiction-variants/) and [Funding & Financing Mechanisms](/guide/funding-financing-mechanisms/).
